# 4. Forgotten in the Prison

Joseph was seventeen when the trouble with his brothers began.

He had been his father's favorite, and he had not been subtle about it. He had dreams in which his brothers bowed down to him, and he told them so. They hated him for it. One day, far from home, they threw him into a dry pit, then sold him to traders on their way to Egypt, and let their father believe he had been killed by an animal.

The next time we hear Joseph's age, he is thirty, standing before Pharaoh. About thirteen years lie between the pit and the palace. Most of the story of Joseph happens in those thirteen years, and most of them were spent as a slave and then a prisoner.

## Doing well, going nowhere

The strange thing about Joseph's middle is how well he did in it.

In Egypt he was bought by Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh. Genesis says that God was with Joseph, and that everything he did went well, so that Potiphar put him in charge of his whole household. Then Potiphar's wife tried, again and again, to seduce him. He refused. She accused him of attacking her, and he was thrown into prison.

In prison, the same pattern repeated. God was with Joseph, the text says, and the keeper of the prison put him in charge of the other prisoners.

I find this both encouraging and unsettling. Encouraging, because it shows that faithfulness in the middle is possible: Joseph worked hard, kept his integrity and cared for the people around him, even as a slave and a prisoner. Unsettling, because it shows that doing the right thing did not get him out. He was faithful, and he was falsely accused. He was faithful, and he stayed in prison.

If you have done everything you know to do, and you are still waiting, you are in Joseph's company. Faithfulness does not always change your circumstances quickly. It may still matter enormously.

## The cupbearer forgot

While in prison, Joseph met two of Pharaoh's officials: the chief cupbearer and the chief baker. Each had a troubling dream, and Joseph explained the dreams to them. The cupbearer, Joseph said, would be restored to his position in three days.

Then Joseph asked him for something. It is one of the few times in the story we hear Joseph speak about his own pain:

Three days later, it happened just as Joseph had said. The cupbearer was restored. Then comes one of the saddest sentences in Genesis:

{{verse: Genesis 40:23}}

And the next chapter begins with a time stamp:

{{verse: Genesis 41:1}}

Two full years. Joseph had done the right thing. He had helped someone. He had a door within reach, a person with access to the king who owed him a favor. And then nothing. Weeks went by, then months. Two whole years.

Many of us know this particular kind of waiting. The interview that went well, and then silence. The friend who promised to put in a word. The doctor who said they would call. The opportunity that seemed so close it had to be the answer, and then quietly was not.

That kind of disappointment can be harder than the original trouble, because it raises our hopes and then drops them. If you are there, I want you to know that the Bible names it. Joseph was forgotten, and the text says so plainly.

## Remembered

After two years, Pharaoh had dreams that no one could interpret, and the cupbearer suddenly remembered. Joseph was brought out of prison, shaved and given fresh clothes, and brought before Pharaoh. He explained the dreams, warned of a coming famine, and was put in charge of preparing Egypt for it.

Years later, during that famine, his brothers came to Egypt to buy food and found themselves bowing before the brother they had sold. When Joseph finally revealed who he was, he wept so loudly that the Egyptians heard it. Later still, after their father died, he said to his brothers:

{{verse: Genesis 50:20}}

This verse is often quoted quickly, and I want to slow it down. Joseph does not say that what his brothers did was good. He says it was evil. He does not say he was glad of the pit, or the prison, or the two years. He says that God was at work through it, toward something good.

He could only say this looking back, many years later, after weeping. He could not have said it in the pit. I do not think it would have helped him to hear it in the prison, from someone who had never been there.

So I will not say it to you as though it explains your waiting. I do not know what God is doing in your story. What Joseph's story shows is that a long, unfair, forgotten middle is not the same thing as a story God has abandoned. Joseph could not see it from inside. Very often, we can't either.

## For the waiting room

2. What does faithfulness look like for you in your middle, today, regardless of whether it changes anything?
