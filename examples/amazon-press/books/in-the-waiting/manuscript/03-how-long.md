# 2. How Long

Many of us learned, somewhere along the way, that there is a right way to talk to God when things are hard. Calm. Grateful. Trusting. Perhaps a little sad, but never angry, never accusing. We learned to tidy our prayers before we said them, the way you might tidy the living room before a guest arrives.

The Bible does not seem to have learned this.

## The prayer of complaint

Read through the book of Psalms and you will find that a large share of them are laments: prayers of complaint, protest and grief. They are not hidden away. They sit right next to the songs of praise, and some of them turn into songs of praise halfway through.

The writers of these psalms tell God they feel abandoned. They ask him why he is asleep. They ask why the wicked do well while the faithful suffer. They describe sleepless nights, tears that soak their beds, bones that ache, enemies who mock them. They say things to God that many of us would be nervous to say out loud in church.

And they keep coming back to two words: *how long*.

That question is a complaint, but it is also a strange kind of faith. You only ask *how long* of someone you believe can change things. You only complain to someone you expect to be listening. A person who had given up on God would not bother. The complaint itself is a way of holding on.

## The turn in Psalm 13

Psalm 13, which we met in the last chapter, is only six verses long. The first four are almost entirely complaint. Then, without explanation, it turns:

{{verse: Psalm 13:5-6}}

Notice what has not changed. Nothing in the psalm tells us that the writer's situation got better. There is no answered prayer between verse four and verse five, no rescue, no news. The enemy is presumably still there. The sorrow is still there.

What changes is where he is looking. In the first half, he is looking at his circumstances and at God's apparent absence. In the second half, he looks back at what he knows about God's character, his loving kindness, and forward to a time when he will rejoice. He decides, in the middle, to trust.

I do not want to make this sound easier than it is. Some days you will get to verse five. Some days you will only get through verse four. Some seasons, you may pray the first half of Psalm 13 for months. That is still prayer. The psalm does not shame the first four verses; it includes them.

## A song in the ruins

There is an even starker example in the book of Lamentations. It was written after the city of Jerusalem was destroyed, its temple burned and many of its people killed or carried off. The book is a series of poems of grief. It does not hold back.

And right in the middle of it, in the third chapter, in the middle of the most painful poem, come these lines:

{{verse: Lamentations 3:22-23}}

Many people know these verses, but they often meet them on a mug or a greeting card, without the ruins around them. In their real setting, they are not a cheerful thought. They are the words of someone sitting in the wreckage of everything they loved, choosing to remember something true about God while nothing around them looked like it.

Even here, the writer does not stop lamenting. After these verses, the poem goes back to grief. The hope is real, but it does not cancel the sorrow. It sits inside it.

## Learning to lament

If you have never prayed this way, it may feel strange, even wrong. Here is a simple shape, taken from the psalms, that you can try:

**Turn to God.** Address him directly, by whatever name you use. Lament is not muttering about God; it is talking to him.

**Say what is wrong.** Plainly. You do not need to dress it up or make it sound spiritual. *I have been out of work for nine months and I am frightened. I don't understand why you haven't helped.*

**Ask for what you want.** Lament is honest about desire. *Please let me find work. Please let this treatment work.*

**Remember something true.** Not something you feel, necessarily, but something you know or have seen. *You have been kind to me before. You have not left me yet.*

**Choose trust, even a small amount.** *I am going to keep coming back to you.* Sometimes that is all the trust you have, and it is enough to say.

You do not need to hit all five every time. You certainly do not need to feel better at the end. The point is not to perform a prayer correctly. It is to stop hiding from God the very thing that is taking up most of your heart.

## For the waiting room

1. Is there something you have been afraid or ashamed to say to God about your waiting? What is it?
2. What is one thing you know to be true about God, even if you do not feel it right now?

*God, how long? I am tired of waiting and I don't understand what you are doing. I am telling you plainly because I believe you are listening. I will try to trust you today, even with this much: [say it]. Amen.*
