# 12. Those Who Wait

The fortieth chapter of Isaiah begins with some of the most tender words in the Bible: comfort, comfort my people. It was written to a people who had suffered a great deal, and who were tired of waiting for God to act.

Toward the end of the chapter, the prophet quotes their complaint back to them:

{{verse: Isaiah 40:27}}

My way is hidden from God. He does not see what is happening to me. My cause has been overlooked.

If you have read this far, you probably know that feeling. It is the feeling of Psalm 13, of Joseph in prison, of Martha on the road. It is the feeling of being forgotten in the middle.

## The God who does not grow tired

Isaiah's answer is not an argument. It is a reminder:

{{verse: Isaiah 40:28-29}}

God does not get tired. His understanding goes deeper than we can search. And he gives strength to people who have none left.

Then come the verses that many people know by heart:

{{verse: Isaiah 40:30-31}}

## Reading the promise carefully

It is worth noticing what this promise says and what it does not.

It does not say that those who wait will get what they are waiting for by a certain date. It says they will renew their strength. The promise is about what God gives *to the person waiting*, not about how quickly the waiting ends.

And look at the order of the three pictures. First, rising up on wings like eagles. Then running without growing weary. Then, finally, walking without fainting.

You might expect it the other way around, building up from walking to running to soaring. But I think the order is wise. Some days in the middle, you will soar: you will feel close to God, full of hope, lifted above it all. Some days you will run: you will have energy to do what needs doing. And many days, perhaps most days, you will walk. Slowly. One foot in front of the other. Not fainting.

That last promise may be the most precious. On the days when all you can do is walk, and not give up, that too is God's strength renewing you. Walking without fainting is not a lesser kind of faith. For a long middle, it may be the main kind.

## Simeon and Anna

I want to end with two people who waited a very long time.

When Jesus was a baby, Mary and Joseph brought him to the temple in Jerusalem, as the law required. Two people there were waiting.

The first was Simeon. Luke tells us he was righteous and devout, and that he was waiting for what he calls the consolation of Israel, the comfort God had promised his people. It had been revealed to him that he would not die before he saw the Messiah. When he saw the child, he took him in his arms and blessed God.

The second was Anna, a prophet. She had been married for seven years and then widowed, and that was a very long time ago. Luke gives the number eighty-four; translations differ on whether that was her age or the years of her widowhood, but either way she was old. She never left the temple, Luke says, but worshipped there with fasting and prayer night and day.

{{verse: Luke 2:38}}

Think about what their waiting looked like. Years and years of ordinary days in the temple. Prayers, fasting, worship. Watching families come and go with their babies. Nothing visibly changing.

And then, one ordinary morning, a young couple from Nazareth walked in with their child.

## The kind of people waiting makes

I do not tell you about Simeon and Anna to promise you that one ordinary morning your answer will walk through the door. It might. It might not. I have tried, throughout this book, not to promise what I do not know.

I tell you about them because of the kind of people they became while they waited. They were not bitter. They were not idle. They were devout and prayerful and faithful. When the moment came, they recognised it, because they had spent their waiting looking toward God. Anna immediately began telling everyone who was waiting with her.

The long middle shapes us. It can make us bitter or it can make us deep. Often it does some of both, on different days. But it does not have to be wasted time. It can be time spent learning to walk without fainting, to keep looking, to keep praying, to stand beside others, and to trust that our way is not hidden from God.

## For the waiting room

1. Is today a soaring day, a running day or a walking day? What would "not fainting" look like today?
2. Looking back over this book, what has helped you most? Write it down so you can find it again.
