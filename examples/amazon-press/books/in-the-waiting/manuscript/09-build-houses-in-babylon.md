# 8. Build Houses in Babylon

There is a verse many Christians know by heart. It is printed on graduation cards and coffee mugs and wall hangings. Perhaps you have one.

{{verse: Jeremiah 29:11}}

It is a beautiful verse. But it was written in a letter, and the letter was written to people in the long middle. When you read the verse in its setting, it becomes, I think, even more beautiful, and much more useful to those of us who are waiting.

## A letter to the exiles

The prophet Jeremiah wrote this letter to people from Jerusalem who had been carried off to Babylon. Their city had been conquered. Their leaders, craftsmen and many families had been deported hundreds of miles from home.

The exiles had a question: how long? Some prophets were telling them it would be short. Soon, they said, God would break the power of Babylon and bring everyone home. Hold on. Don't unpack.

Jeremiah's letter said something different. It would not be soon. A little further on in the same chapter, he tells them the exile would last seventy years. Most of the adults who read the letter would not live to see the end of it.

And so, before the famous verse about a hope and a future, the letter gives some very down-to-earth instructions:

{{verse: Jeremiah 29:5-7}}

## Don't put your life on hold

Read it slowly. Build houses. Plant gardens. Eat what you grow. Get married. Have children, and let your children marry. Grow in number. Seek the peace and the good of the city where you are, even this city, Babylon, the city of your conquerors. Pray for it.

In other words: *live*. Don't wait on the platform with your suitcases packed for a train that is not coming soon. Unpack. Put down roots. Make a real life in the place where you actually are.

This is one of the most practical words in the Bible for anyone in a long middle. When we are waiting for something, it is very tempting to put life on hold until it arrives. *I'll start enjoying things again once I have a job. I'll make friends once I know whether we're moving. I'll take care of myself once the diagnosis is clear. I'll get involved at church once this season is over.*

But if the season is long, a life on hold is a life lost. Jeremiah's letter says to the exiles: this is your life too. Not the life you would have chosen, not the life you are hoping for, but a real one. God is with you in Babylon. Build something here.

## The good of the place you are in

The exiles were also told to seek the good of the city. Not just to survive in it, but to pray for it and work for its peace.

Waiting tends to turn us inward. Our own need gets so loud that it is hard to see anyone else. That is understandable, and you should not feel guilty for it. But there is often some relief, and some meaning, in looking outward again, even a little. Who is around you in this season? Who else is waiting? What good could you do, right where you are, that you would never have had the chance to do if your life had gone to plan?

Some people do their kindest work in the middle of their own long waits. Not because they have it all together, but because they decide their waiting will not be the whole of their story.

## Then the promise

Only after all this does the letter say that God knows the thoughts he thinks toward them (many translations say "plans"): thoughts of peace and not of evil, to give them hope and a future. And then it goes on: you will call on me, and pray to me, and I will listen. You will seek me and find me when you search with all your heart.

In its setting, this is a promise to a whole people over a long span of time. It does not promise each reader a quick return, or a particular outcome. Many who first read it died in Babylon. The hope was real, and it was for their children and their people, and it rested in God's faithfulness, not in their circumstances changing next month.

So I would not read this verse as a guarantee that you will get what you are waiting for, on the timeline you want. But I would read it as this: the God who asked his people to build houses in Babylon did not forget them there. He had not stopped thinking of them. He was with them in exile, and he invited them to seek him there.

## For the waiting room

1. Where have you put your life on hold while you wait? What is one way you could "build a house" or "plant a garden" in this season?
2. Who around you could you seek the good of, right where you are?

*God, I don't want to be here, and I don't know how long it will last. Help me to live a real life in this place anyway. Show me what to build, whom to love, and how to seek the good of where I am. Amen.*
