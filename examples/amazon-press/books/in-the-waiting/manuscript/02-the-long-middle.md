# 1. The Long Middle

Every story has a middle, and the middle is the part nobody talks about.

We tell beginnings well. *I lost my job. We started trying for a baby. The scan showed a shadow.* We tell endings well, too. *And then the call came. And then she was born. And then the results were clear.* At church, the stories that get told from the front are usually the ones with endings. They are good stories, and we are right to give thanks for them.

But most of life is lived in the middle. The middle is the eleventh month of sending out applications. It is the fourth year of a marriage that is not getting better. It is the hospital car park, the empty room, the date on the calendar that comes around again. In the middle, nobody asks you to share your testimony, because you do not have one yet. You have a prayer that has not been answered, and a life that has to be lived anyway.

## What makes the middle hard

Waiting is hard partly because of the thing you want. If you are waiting for a job, money is tight and your days have lost their shape. If you are waiting for healing, you are in pain. Those are real losses, and you do not need to pretend otherwise.

But waiting also brings its own particular troubles, ones that pile on top of the thing itself.

There is **not knowing**. A known loss, however hard, can be grieved. A loss that might not be a loss, that might turn around next week, keeps you in a kind of suspended breath. You cannot fully hope and you cannot fully let go.

There is **time**. Waiting stretches days out. Every morning starts with the same question, and every evening ends with the same answer: not today.

There is **comparison**. Other people's prayers seem to be answered. A friend gets the promotion. Your sister announces her pregnancy. Someone in your small group shares how God provided, and you are glad for them, truly, and also something in you sinks.

And there is **silence**. This is often the hardest part for people of faith. You have prayed. You have asked others to pray. You have looked for signs. And heaven seems quiet.

## You are not the first

Here is something worth holding on to: the Bible is full of the middle.

Abraham was told he would be the father of a great nation, and then waited about twenty-five years for one son. Joseph had dreams of leadership and then spent years as a slave and a prisoner. Hannah prayed for a child year after year while another woman mocked her. The people of Israel spent generations in Egypt, then forty years in the wilderness, then decades in exile in Babylon. The psalms are full of people asking God how long, and the prophets are full of people waiting for promises they would not live to see. In the New Testament, Simeon and Anna waited in the temple well into old age for the one God had promised.

Some of these people saw the thing they waited for. Some did not. We will look at both kinds, because both are in the book, and because I do not want to offer you only the stories with happy endings.

What strikes me about all of them is how *ordinary* their waiting was. They got up in the morning. They worked. They argued with God. They made bad decisions out of impatience. They had friends who helped and friends who did not. Their middles looked a lot like ours.

## God is in the middle too

One of the oldest prayers of waiting in the Bible is Psalm 13. It is short, and it begins like this:

{{verse: Psalm 13:1-2}}

Four times, the writer asks the same question. *How long?* He feels forgotten. He feels that God's face is turned away. He is tired of his own anxious thoughts going round and round, and tired of the sorrow that comes back every day.

This prayer is in the Bible. Not in a footnote, not as an example of what not to do, but in the hymnbook of God's people, meant to be sung together. That tells me something. It tells me that God is not embarrassed by the middle, and does not expect us to be either.

We will come back to Psalm 13 in the next chapter, because it does not end where it starts. But for now, notice simply this: the person who wrote it was in the middle, and he was praying. He had not received an answer. He did not feel close to God. He prayed anyway, and he prayed honestly.

That is where we start. Not with having it all worked out. Not with feeling peaceful. Just with turning toward God, from the middle, and saying where we are.

## For the waiting room

1. What are you waiting for? Try to write it in one plain sentence, without explaining or softening it.
2. Which part of waiting is hardest for you right now: not knowing, time, comparison, or silence? Something else?

*God, I am in the middle. I asked you for something a long time ago, and I am still waiting. I do not know how this ends. I am telling you where I am because I want you to be here with me. Amen.*
