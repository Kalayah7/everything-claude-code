# 3. Twenty-Five Years

When God first spoke to Abraham, he was not yet called Abraham. He was Abram, he was seventy-five years old, and he had no children.

The promise was enormous. Leave your country, God told him, and I will make you into a great nation. Abram went. The book of Genesis tells us, quite simply, that he left as he had been told, and that he was seventy-five when he did.

It would be about twenty-five years before his son Isaac was born.

## What twenty-five years looks like

It is easy to read the story of Abraham in an afternoon and miss how long it took. The chapters move quickly. A promise, a journey, a famine, a quarrel, another promise. Turn a few pages and Isaac is born.

But twenty-five years is a long time. Think back twenty-five years in your own life. Think about how many birthdays, how many ordinary Tuesdays, how many nights lying awake fit into that space. Abraham and Sarah lived every one of those days as a couple with a promise and no child of their own.

And they were not young. Each year that passed made the promise look less likely, not more. By the time a visitor came to their tent and said Sarah would have a son within a year, she was well past the age of bearing children. She was listening from the tent door, and she laughed.

{{verse: Genesis 18:12}}

I find that laugh one of the most human moments in the Bible. It is not the laugh of someone who finds something funny. It is the laugh of someone who has hoped for too long and been disappointed too many times to hope again. It is a laugh that protects her heart.

If you have ever braced yourself against good news, refused to get excited because you could not bear another letdown, you know that laugh. Sarah is your company.

## The shortcut

There is another part of Abraham and Sarah's story that is less comfortable to read.

About ten years after they arrived in Canaan, still with no child, Sarai (as she was then called) came up with a plan. She gave her Egyptian servant Hagar to Abram, so that Hagar might have a child for her. This was a known practice in their culture. It must have looked sensible. God had promised Abram descendants; perhaps this was how it would happen.

Hagar did conceive, and the result was misery. Sarai and Hagar turned against each other. Sarai treated Hagar harshly, and Hagar fled into the wilderness while pregnant. Years later the rivalry between the two women and their sons ended with Hagar and her son Ishmael being sent away.

It would be easy to turn this into a neat moral about impatience, but Sarai was not foolish. She was a woman who had waited a decade for a promise that seemed to be addressed mainly to her husband, in a world where a woman's worth was tied to children. Her plan came out of real pain.

But the story is honest about what happened. When waiting goes on long enough, we are tempted to make the promise happen ourselves, by whatever means are at hand. Sometimes that is wise and good: applying for jobs, seeing a specialist, having a hard conversation. Sometimes it is a shortcut that hurts other people and ourselves.

The question is not whether we act. It is what we are willing to do, and who we are willing to hurt, to get out of the middle.

## Meeting God in the wilderness

There is a quiet grace inside this messy story. When Hagar ran away, pregnant and alone, an angel found her by a spring in the wilderness. He spoke to her by name, told her that God had heard her misery, and told her to name her son Ishmael, which means "God hears".

{{verse: Genesis 16:13}}

Hagar was not the one the promise was given to. She was the one hurt by someone else's impatience. And yet God saw her too, in the middle of her own wilderness.

If your waiting has led you to make decisions you regret, or if you have been hurt by someone else's shortcut, this story is for you as much as anyone. The God of Abraham's long middle is also the God who sees the people at the edges of the story.

## The promise kept

Isaac was born when Abraham was a hundred years old. This time Sarah said God had made her laugh, a very different laughter from the bitter kind, and that everyone who heard would laugh with her. Isaac's name means "he laughs".

I do not tell you this ending to promise you the same ending. I do not know what God will do in your story. Abraham and Sarah received what they had been promised, in one son, at the very end of what seemed possible. Others in the Bible did not receive in their lifetimes what they waited for, and we will come to them.

What I take from Abraham and Sarah is not a formula, but company. People of great faith waited for a very long time. They doubted. They laughed bitterly. They tried shortcuts. God did not give up on them in the middle. Their story is told as part of the family history of faith, warts and all.

## For the waiting room

1. Have you ever laughed like Sarah, protecting yourself from hope? What would it cost you to hope again, a little?
2. Is there a shortcut you are tempted to take right now? Who else would it affect?

*God who sees me, you were patient with Abraham and Sarah through all their long years and their mistakes. Be patient with me. Show me which steps are mine to take, and which are only mine to wait for. Amen.*
