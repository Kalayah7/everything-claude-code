# 5. Year After Year

Every year, Elkanah took his family up to the town of Shiloh to worship and offer sacrifices. And every year, the trip broke his wife's heart.

Elkanah had two wives. Peninnah had children. Hannah had none. The first book of Samuel tells us that Peninnah provoked Hannah, deliberately, to make her miserable, and that this happened year after year, every time they went up to the house of the Lord. Hannah would weep and refuse to eat.

## The pain on the calendar

There is something very true about this detail. Waiting is often hardest at particular times of year. Holidays. Anniversaries. A due date that came and went. The first day of school for other people's children. Family gatherings where someone always asks the question.

For Hannah, it was the annual festival, the one meant to be the most joyful time of the year. It was also the time she was surrounded by Peninnah's children and Peninnah's cruelty.

Elkanah loved her. He gave her a double portion at the feast. He also said something that many well-meaning people have said since:

{{verse: 1 Samuel 1:8}}

He meant to comfort her. But I suspect it did not help. He was asking her to measure her grief against his love, as if one should cancel out the other. It does not work that way. You can be deeply loved and still deeply grieve something you do not have.

If people who love you have tried to fix your sadness, or measure it, or hurry it along, Hannah knows how that feels.

## Pouring out

One year, after the meal, Hannah got up and went to the house of the Lord. She was, the text says, in bitterness of soul, and she prayed and wept bitterly.

She made a vow: if God would give her a son, she would give him back to God for all his life.

Eli, the priest, was sitting by the doorpost. He watched her lips moving with no sound coming out, and he decided she was drunk. He told her to put away her wine.

This is painful to read. Hannah, at her most desperate and most honest, is misjudged by the religious leader in the room. Many people who have prayed hard in public, or wept in church, or confessed their struggle to someone they trusted, know what that feels like.

Hannah answered him:

{{verse: 1 Samuel 1:15-16}}

*Poured out my soul.* That phrase is the heart of this chapter. Hannah did not offer God a tidy request. She emptied herself out before him, all of it: the grief, the years, the humiliation, the longing.

## Before the answer

Then something happened that I find remarkable. Eli, now understanding, blessed her and said he hoped God would grant her request. And Hannah went her way:

{{verse: 1 Samuel 1:18}}

Look at the order. Hannah's face changed *before* anything else changed. She was not yet pregnant. She had no guarantee. Eli had expressed a hope, not made a promise. Yet after pouring out her heart, she was able to eat again, and her face was no longer sad.

I do not think this means her longing disappeared. I think it means something was lifted from her by the act of being honest before God. She had carried this alone, or among people who misunderstood it, for years. Now she had given it to God, fully, and she could walk away and eat a meal.

Hannah did go on to have a son, Samuel, who became one of the great prophets of Israel. She kept her vow and brought him to serve in the house of the Lord. Later she had other children. Her prayer of praise at the start of chapter two is one of the great songs in the Bible.

Not everyone who prays as Hannah prayed is given a child, and the Bible does not promise it. If that is your grief, chapter 10 is for you too.

But I want you to notice that part of her relief came before the answer. Honest prayer did not get her what she wanted on the spot. It did something to her in the middle.

## What to do with your grief

If your waiting has a calendar, if some days are much harder than others, consider marking them. Know they are coming. Plan something gentle for yourself, or tell a friend in advance that this day is difficult. You do not need to pretend those days are ordinary.

And if you have never poured out your soul to God, all of it, perhaps try. Find a place where you will not be overheard. Say everything, or write everything. Do not edit it. You may be surprised, as Hannah may have been, at what changes in you, even when nothing else has changed yet.

## For the waiting room

1. Which days or seasons are hardest in your waiting? How could you prepare for the next one?
2. If you poured out your whole heart to God right now, what would you say? Try it.

*God, here is everything. [Say it, or write it.] I am pouring it out because I can't carry it by myself any longer. Lift what you can from me today, even before anything changes. Amen.*
