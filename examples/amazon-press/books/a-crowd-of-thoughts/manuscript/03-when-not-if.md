# 2. When, Not If

The heading of Psalm 56 links it to a frightening moment in David's life: the time the Philistines seized him in the city of Gath.

The story behind it is in the first book of Samuel. David was on the run from King Saul, who wanted him dead. With nowhere safe to go, he fled to Gath, a Philistine city, home of the giant he had once killed. The servants of the king of Gath recognized him. David, the text says, became very afraid. To save his life, he pretended to be mad, scratching at the doors and letting saliva run down his beard, until the king asked why they had brought this madman to him.

It is not a heroic scene. It is the scene of a terrified man doing whatever he could think of to survive.

## The honesty of "when"

Out of that experience, or one like it, comes this line:

{{verse: Psalm 56:3}}

Read it slowly. David does not say, "*If* I am ever afraid." He does not say, "I am never afraid, because I trust you." He says *when*. Fear was part of his life. He expected it to come again.

Many of us have absorbed a picture of faith in which fear and trust cannot live in the same heart. If we are afraid, we must not be trusting. If we were trusting, we would not be afraid. So when fear comes, as it does, we conclude that our faith is broken.

David's prayer does not work that way. In this verse, fear and trust are in the same sentence, in the same moment. Trust is not the absence of fear. It is what David does *with* his fear. He is afraid, and he puts his trust in God.

## Trust as a direction, not a feeling

This is a relief for anxious people, because it means trust does not depend on how you feel.

You may not be able to make the fear go away. Anxiety often runs in the body as much as in the mind: a racing heart, tight muscles, a churning stomach. You cannot argue your nervous system into calm with a verse.

But you can turn. Trust, in David's prayer, is more like a direction than a feeling. It is facing toward God while the fear is still there. Sometimes that means a whole prayer. Sometimes it means one sentence said through clenched teeth. *I am afraid, and I am trusting you.* Sometimes it means simply not giving up on God, staying in the conversation.

A little later in the same psalm, David says something remarkable:

{{verse: Psalm 56:8}}

God keeps count of his wanderings. God keeps his tears. In David's imagination, none of his frightened nights are wasted or forgotten. They are recorded, kept and seen.

## Practicing "when"

Here is a small practice for anxious days. When fear rises, try saying David's verse, or your own version of it, out loud or under your breath. *When I am afraid, I will put my trust in you.* Not to make the fear vanish, but to give it a direction.

You might also find it helpful to write a short note to yourself, for the next time you are afraid. *When the fear comes back, remember: it is allowed. You have been afraid before and God was still there. You can be afraid and still turn toward him.* Keep it somewhere you will find it.

David was afraid often enough to write a psalm about it. He was also, the Bible says, a man after God's own heart. Both are true. They can both be true of you.

## For the quiet minute

1. What are you afraid of right now? Write it in one plain sentence.
2. What would it look like to trust God with that while you are still afraid?

*God, I am afraid. I am not going to pretend otherwise. And while I am afraid, I am turning toward you. Keep count of my tears. Do not let go of me. Amen.*
