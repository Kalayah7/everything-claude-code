# 4. In Chains

If you have struggled with anxiety for any length of time, someone has probably quoted this verse to you:

{{verse: Philippians 4:6}}

*In nothing be anxious.* For many anxious Christians, these are some of the hardest words in the Bible. They can sound like a command to stop feeling something you cannot simply switch off. And when you fail to obey, the verse becomes one more thing to feel anxious about.

So it is worth looking carefully at who wrote it, where, and what else he said.

## Written in chains

Paul wrote the letter to the Philippians while he was a prisoner. Early in the letter he mentions his chains, and that the whole palace guard knows he is imprisoned for Christ. He did not know whether he would be released or executed. He writes openly about both possibilities.

Paul was writing as a man with every reason in the world to be anxious, to a church that was anxious about him.

And Paul was no stranger to anxiety himself. In another letter, after listing the beatings, shipwrecks, hunger and dangers he had faced, he adds this:

{{verse: 2 Corinthians 11:28}}

The same apostle who wrote *in nothing be anxious* admitted that he carried anxiety every day. He was not describing a life without worry. He was describing what to do with it.

## What the verse actually says

Look again at Philippians 4:6. It does not end after *in nothing be anxious*. It goes straight on: *but in everything, by prayer and petition with thanksgiving, let your requests be made known to God.*

The verse is not mainly a prohibition. It is a redirection. Instead of carrying the worry around alone, turning it over and over, take it to God. *In everything.* Not only the big, respectable worries, but everything. Tell him what you want. Ask.

And then comes the next verse:

{{verse: Philippians 4:7}}

Notice what the promise is. It does not say the thing you are worried about will go away. It does not say you will never feel anxious again. It says that God's peace, a peace beyond understanding, will *guard* your heart and your mind.

A guard is posted at a gate because there is something outside that might get in. The image assumes that trouble is still around. God's peace does not always remove the danger. It stands guard over you in the middle of it.

## With thanksgiving

The verse also mentions thanksgiving, and this, too, can be misheard. It does not mean pretending to be grateful for the thing that frightens you. It means bringing your requests alongside an honest look at what is still good.

For an anxious mind, this can be a genuine help. Anxiety narrows our attention to threats. It is very good at noticing everything that might go wrong, and very poor at noticing what is going right. Thanksgiving widens the view a little. *I am frightened about the appointment. Thank you for the friend who is driving me. Thank you for the coffee this morning. Thank you that you are here.*

It does not erase the fear. It sets it in a larger picture.

## A kinder reading

So, if Philippians 4:6 has felt like a stick to beat yourself with, try reading it as Paul may have meant it: as an invitation from one anxious believer in chains to others. *Do not carry this alone. Bring all of it to God. Thank him for what is still good. And his peace will stand guard over you, even here.*

## For the quiet minute

1. Has Philippians 4:6 ever made you feel worse? What difference does it make to know that Paul wrote it in prison and admitted his own daily anxiety?
2. Make a short list of your requests, and beside them, a short list of things you are thankful for today.

*God, I am anxious about [name it]. Here is what I am asking for: [ask]. And here is what I am thankful for, even now: [name it]. Let your peace stand guard over my heart and mind. Amen.*
