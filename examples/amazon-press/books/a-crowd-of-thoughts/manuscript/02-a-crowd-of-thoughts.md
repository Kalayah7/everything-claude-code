# 1. A Crowd of Thoughts

There is a line in the ninety-fourth psalm that anxious people tend to recognize at once:

{{verse: Psalm 94:19}}

Notice the phrase *the multitude of my thoughts*. A multitude. A crowd. Not one worry, neatly labeled, but a whole crowd of them, jostling, talking over each other, refusing to leave.

## The crowd is in the Bible

It is worth pausing on the simple fact that this line is in Scripture. The person who wrote it had a mind that ran away with them. They did not hide it. They wrote it into a prayer that God's people would sing for thousands of years.

Psalm 94 is not a calm psalm. It is a prayer for justice from someone surrounded by cruelty: people who crush the weak and say that God does not see. The writer is upset, and with good reason. Their anxious thoughts are not irrational. They are the natural response of someone who sees real wrong and feels helpless in the face of it.

That matters, because anxious people are often told that their worries are silly. Some are, of course. But many are not. People worry about money because bills are real. They worry about their children because the world really is dangerous. They worry about their health because bodies really do fail. Anxiety often fastens onto something true and then magnifies it, replays it, and will not put it down.

The psalm does not pretend the crowd is not there. It does not say, "When my thoughts were few, your comforts delighted my soul." It says that *in* the crowd, while it was still there, comfort came.

## Comfort in the middle of the noise

That order is important. Many of us think we need to clear our minds before we can pray. We wait until we feel settled, or we try to push the thoughts away, and when we cannot, we give up, feeling that we have failed at prayer as well as everything else.

The psalm suggests a different way. You can start in the middle of the noise. You can bring the whole crowd with you.

In practice, that might look like this. Instead of trying to empty your mind, you name what is in it. *God, I am worried about the test results. I am worried about what my sister said. I am worried about the car. I am worried I left the stove on. I am worried that I am always worried.* The list can be long. It can be messy. It can include things you are embarrassed to worry about.

Something often happens when the crowd is named before God. It does not necessarily disperse. But it becomes a crowd you are standing in *with* someone, rather than alone. And sometimes, among all those voices, you begin to hear a quieter one.

## What comfort looks like

The psalm says God's comforts *delight* the soul. That is a strong word. It does not promise that every worry is answered. In the very next verses, the writer is still asking for justice. But it says that, in the crowd, something good reached them. Something that did not merely numb the anxiety but brought a kind of joy alongside it.

What were those comforts? The verses around it name some of them. When the writer felt their foot slipping, God's loving kindness held them up. God was their refuge, the rock they could hide in. These are not abstract ideas. They are pictures of being held, of having somewhere to go.

For you, comfort might come as a verse that steadies you, a friend's text at the right moment, an hour of sleep, a memory of a time God was faithful, or simply the sense, faint but real, that you are not alone in the noise. These are small things. They are also, according to this psalm, the kind of things God gives.

## For the quiet minute

1. What thoughts are crowding your mind today? List them without sorting them or explaining them.
2. What has comforted you before, even briefly, when your mind was this full?

*God, my mind is crowded. Here is what is in it: [name them]. I am bringing the whole crowd to you, because I do not know how to clear it. Comfort me in the middle of it. Amen.*
