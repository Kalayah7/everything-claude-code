# 8. The Lilies and the Birds

In the middle of the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus talks about worry for longer than almost anywhere else in the Gospels. He was speaking to ordinary people, many of them poor, for whom food and clothing were not small concerns. And he says this:

{{verse: Matthew 6:25}}

It would be easy to hear this as dismissive: *stop worrying about food and clothes, there are more important things.* But Jesus does not stop there. He points to two things in front of his listeners.

## Look at the birds

{{verse: Matthew 6:26}}

Look at the birds. It is an instruction to pay attention, to use your eyes. Somewhere nearby, as Jesus spoke, birds were flying or perched or pecking at the ground. They do not plant or harvest or store grain. And yet they are fed.

Then he turns to the flowers of the field, the wild lilies. They do not work or spin, he says, and yet not even King Solomon in all his splendor was dressed like one of them. If God clothes the grass, which is here today and thrown into the oven tomorrow, how much more will he clothe you?

## An argument from attention

What Jesus is doing here is gently interrupting the anxious mind.

Anxiety lives in the future. It runs ahead to tomorrow, next month, next year, and fills the space with possibilities, most of them bad. It is almost never in the present moment, looking at what is actually here.

*Look at the birds. Consider the lilies.* These are invitations to come back to now. To notice, with your actual eyes, something small and alive that God is caring for at this moment.

This is no trick to make worry disappear. Jesus is making an argument: the God who pays attention to birds and wildflowers is paying attention to you. You are, as he says, of much more value than they are. But the argument works best when you actually look. When you stand at a window and watch a bird for a full minute, or kneel and examine a flower, something in the body can begin to slow down, and the truth Jesus is pointing to can land.

## One moment

In the middle of this passage, Jesus asks a simple, almost humorous question:

{{verse: Matthew 6:27}}

Worry promises a great deal. It feels like it is doing something useful: preparing, protecting, solving. But Jesus points out that it cannot add even a single moment to your life (many translations say "a single hour"). All that effort, and nothing is added.

Rather than a scolding, this is a kind of release. If worry cannot add a moment, then you are not failing in your duty when you set it down for a while. You are not leaving your loved ones unprotected if you stop rehearsing every disaster. The work of keeping the world turning was never yours.

## Seek first

The passage ends with an invitation and a reassurance. Seek first God's kingdom and his righteousness, Jesus says, and all these things will be given to you as well. And then: do not be anxious for tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Each day has enough trouble of its own.

Tomorrow will have its own trouble. You only have to live today.

## A practice: look and consider

The next time you feel anxiety rising, try Jesus's two instructions literally.

**Look.** Find something living: a bird, a tree, a houseplant, a pet, a patch of sky. Look at it for one full minute. Notice its color, its movement, its detail.

**Consider.** As you look, remind yourself: God is caring for this, right now. And I am of more value to him than this.

It takes one minute. It will not solve your problems. But it may give your mind a place to rest, and your heart a truth to hold.

## For the quiet minute

1. Go and look at something living for one minute. What did you notice?
2. What worry has felt like it was "doing something useful"? What would it be like to set it down for today?

*God who feeds the birds and dresses the flowers, I am worried about tomorrow. Bring me back to today. Help me see how you are caring for the small things around me, and trust that you are caring for me too. Amen.*
