# 5. Cast It

The first letter of Peter was written to Christians scattered across what is now Turkey, people who were facing suspicion and hostility because of their faith. Near the end of the letter comes this short sentence:

{{verse: 1 Peter 5:7}}

It is one of the simplest verses about anxiety in the Bible. It has two parts: an action and a reason.

## The action: cast

The word *cast* is a strong, physical word. In the Gospels, a closely related word is used when the disciples throw their cloaks onto the colt for Jesus to ride into Jerusalem. It is not a gentle setting down. It is a throw.

That picture can help an anxious mind. Worry tends to be something we hold onto tightly, as if letting go of it would be careless. If I stop worrying about my son, something might happen to him. If I stop going over the presentation, I might fail. Part of us believes the worry itself is keeping things safe.

Peter says: throw it. Take the weight off your own shoulders and put it onto God's. Not because the thing does not matter, but because it matters too much for you to carry alone.

And Peter says *all* your worries. Not only the respectable ones. Not only the ones you think God would be interested in. All of them.

## The reason: he cares

The second half of the verse gives the reason. *Because he cares for you.*

That is the whole argument. Not because worry is a sin. Not because you should be stronger. Not because God is tired of hearing about it. Because he cares for you.

Many anxious people carry a hidden fear that God is impatient with them: that they have brought the same worries to him too many times, that he must be sighing at them by now. Peter's reason is the answer to that fear. You cast your worries on God *because* he cares for you, and the casting is welcome, as often as you need to do it.

## Casting more than once

Here is something worth saying plainly: you will probably have to cast the same worry many times.

You will throw it onto God in the morning, and by lunchtime you will find you have picked it up again. You will give it to him at night, and at three in the morning it will be back on your chest. This does not mean you did it wrong. It is what anxious minds do. They pick things back up.

So cast it again. And again. Each time is a real act of trust, not a failure. Over time, many people find that the worry sits a little more lightly, or that they notice sooner when they have picked it back up.

## A practice: the casting list

Some people find it helpful to make casting physical. Here is one way:

Take a sheet of paper. Write down every worry on your mind, one per line. Do not tidy them. Then, for each one, write beside it: *I give this to you, because you care for me.*

When you have finished, put the paper somewhere out of sight, in a drawer or a box. If you like, you can label the box with this verse. When a worry returns, remind yourself that you have already given it away, and give it away again.

This is not magic. The worries are still real. But the practice gives your body and mind something concrete to do with them, other than carrying them.

## For the quiet minute

1. Which worry are you holding most tightly right now? What do you fear might happen if you let go of it?
2. Do you believe God cares for you? What makes that hard or easy to believe today?
