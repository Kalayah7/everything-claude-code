# 11. Whatever Is True

Anxious minds are very good storytellers.

A friend does not reply to a message, and within an hour the mind has written a whole story: she is angry with me, I said something wrong, the friendship is ending. A small ache becomes a serious illness. A late train becomes a missed meeting, a lost job, an unpaid mortgage. Each story feels completely real while we are inside it.

Paul knew something about the stories our minds tell. In the same letter where he wrote about anxiety and God's guarding peace, he went on to write this:

{{verse: Philippians 4:8}}

## Not just positive thinking

This verse is sometimes used as if it means *think happy thoughts*. But that is not what it says.

The first item on the list is *whatever things are true*. Not whatever is pleasant, or whatever is comforting, but whatever is true. Paul is not asking anyone to pretend. He is asking them to dwell on reality, to let their minds rest on what is actually so.

For an anxious mind, this is an important distinction. Anxiety does not usually lie outright. It takes something that might happen and treats it as something that will. It takes a small piece of the truth and builds a frightening story on top of it. The answer is not to replace it with a cheerful fantasy. It is to come back to what is true.

## Checking the story

Here is a simple way to practice this, one that many people find useful. When you notice your mind spinning a frightening story, pause and ask three questions:

**What do I actually know?** Not what I fear, or what I imagine, but what I know. *My friend has not replied for two hours.* That is all.

**What else could be true?** *She might be busy. Her phone might be off. She might be thinking about how to reply.* You do not have to believe these, just notice that they are possible.

**What is true about God, right now?** *He is with me. He cares for me. He is not anxious about this.*

These questions will not always make the anxiety go away. But they gently loosen the story's grip, and they bring your mind back toward the kind of thinking Paul describes.

## Honorable, just, pure, lovely

The rest of Paul's list widens the view. Whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is of good report, anything excellent or worthy of praise.

Anxiety narrows our attention. It locks onto threats and blocks out almost everything else. Paul's list is like opening the curtains. There is more in the world than the thing you fear. There is kindness, beauty, good work, people who love you, music, the smell of rain. These are true too.

*Think about these things.* The word means to consider carefully, to let the mind stay on something. It is a practice, not a single decision. You will drift back to the frightening stories, probably many times a day. Each time you notice, you can gently bring your mind back.

## The God of peace

Paul ends this section with a promise that echoes the one a few verses earlier:

{{verse: Philippians 4:9}}

A few verses before, it was *the peace of God* that would guard your heart. Here it is *the God of peace* who will be with you. Not just a feeling of peace, but the person of God himself, present with you as you practice turning your mind toward what is true.

## For the quiet minute

1. What frightening story has your mind been telling lately? What do you actually know, and what else could be true?
2. Name three things that are true, lovely or worthy of praise in your life today.

*God of peace, my mind tells frightening stories. Help me come back to what is true: about my situation, about the good things around me, and about you. Be with me as I practice. Amen.*
