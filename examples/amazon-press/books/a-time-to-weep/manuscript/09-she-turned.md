# 8. She Turned

Early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene went to the tomb where Jesus had been buried.

She had followed him. She had watched him die. And now, at the first possible moment after the Sabbath, she went to be near his body. It was the kind of thing grieving people do: going to the grave, needing to be where the one you lost was last.

The stone had been rolled away. The body was gone.

## Weeping outside the tomb

Mary ran to tell the disciples. Peter and another disciple came running, looked inside, and went home. But Mary stayed.

{{verse: John 20:11}}

Her grief had just been made worse: not only was Jesus dead, but now even his body was gone. She could not even do the last loving thing for him.

Two angels in white asked her why she was weeping. She said, *They have taken away my Lord, and I don't know where they have laid him.* Then she turned around and saw a man standing there. He asked her the same question. Thinking he was the gardener, she said, *Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away.*

## Her name

Then he said one word:

{{verse: John 20:16}}

He said her name. *Mary.* And she knew him.

The risen Jesus did not first announce his victory over death, or explain the resurrection, or correct her mistake. He called her by her name. In the middle of her weeping, she was known.

## Hope, not hurry

Christians believe that the resurrection of Jesus is the ground of their hope for everyone who dies trusting him: that death is not the end, and that one day there will be a reunion. That hope is real, and it is at the center of the Christian faith.

But notice how the story holds it. Jesus did not scold Mary for weeping. Her tears were not a lack of faith. She wept right up to the moment she heard her name.

You can hold Christian hope and still weep. You can believe in the resurrection and still miss someone so much it takes your breath away. Hope does not hurry your grief. It holds it.

This book will not tell you where your loved one is now. That is not something any book can know about a particular person, and it belongs to God. What this story says is that the one who conquered death knows the names of the grieving. He knew Mary's. He knows yours.

## When you are ready

Where do you go, or what do you do, to feel near the one you lost? What would it mean to hear your own name spoken by God in your grief?

*Jesus, you called Mary by her name while she was weeping. I am weeping too. Call me by my name. Let me know that I am known. Amen.*
