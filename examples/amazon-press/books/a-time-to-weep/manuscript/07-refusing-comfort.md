# 6. Refusing Comfort

There is a line in the prophet Jeremiah that is hard to read and important to know:

{{verse: Jeremiah 31:15}}

Rachel was one of the mothers of Israel, wife of Jacob. Centuries after her death, Jeremiah pictures her weeping for her children, the people of Israel who had been killed or carried off into exile. She weeps, and she refuses to be comforted, because they are no more.

The Gospel of Matthew quotes these words again, in one of the darkest moments in the New Testament: when King Herod ordered the killing of the young boys of Bethlehem. Rachel's weeping is the Bible's way of naming a grief so deep that no words can reach it.

## When comfort does not comfort

There is something important in that phrase, *refuses to be comforted*. It is not presented as a failure. It is presented as the natural response to unbearable loss.

Many grieving people find that comfort does not comfort. Kind words bounce off. Verses that once helped feel empty. Others try to cheer you up, and you feel further away from them, not closer. You may even feel angry at the people trying to help.

That does not mean you are doing grief wrong. It may mean your loss is too large to be comforted yet. Rachel's weeping is in the Bible. Grief that refuses easy comfort has a place in Scripture, and in God's sight.

## Loss of a child

This chapter needs to say something especially to parents who have lost a child, at any age, and to those who have lost a pregnancy or a baby. Rachel weeping for her children is, for many such parents, the most honest picture of their grief in the whole Bible.

If that is you, your loss is real, whether your child lived for decades or days or not at all outside the womb. You do not have to be comforted on anyone else's schedule. And you do not have to accept words that make your child's death sound like part of a neat plan.

Many parents find it helps to be with others who have suffered the same kind of loss. Some churches and hospitals offer groups for bereaved parents and for pregnancy and infant loss. A counselor who specializes in grief can also help.

## God hears the weeping

In Jeremiah, after Rachel's weeping, God speaks to her. In its setting, his words are about her children, the exiles, coming home again. The context is the history of a nation, and it is not a promise about any individual family. But one thing in the passage is true for every grieving parent: God hears Rachel. Her weeping is not ignored. It reaches him.

You do not have to stop weeping for him to hear you. He hears the weeping itself.

## When you are ready

Have you felt that comfort does not comfort? What, if anything, has reached you at all?

*God, I cannot be comforted right now. You heard Rachel weeping. Hear me. I do not need answers today. I need to know that you hear. Amen.*
