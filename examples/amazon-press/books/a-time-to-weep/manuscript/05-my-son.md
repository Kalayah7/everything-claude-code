# 4. My Son

Few words in the Bible are as painful as a father's cry for his son.

King David had a son named Absalom. Their relationship was not simple. Absalom had killed his half-brother, gone into exile, been brought back, and then turned against his father. He won over the people and led a rebellion, and David had to flee Jerusalem for his life.

When David's army went out to fight Absalom's forces, David gave his commanders one instruction: deal gently with the young man, for my sake. But in the battle, Absalom's head was caught in the branches of a great oak, and David's commander Joab and his men killed him.

## Grief for a complicated love

David's grief is raw and repetitive. *My son, my son.* He says he wishes he had died instead. There is no composure here, no theology, no dignity. Just a father, alone in a room above a gate, crying for his child.

What makes this story especially important is that the relationship was broken. Absalom had betrayed David, humiliated him and tried to take his throne. If anyone had reason to feel his grief was complicated, it was David.

Many people grieve someone with whom things were not simple: a parent who hurt them, a sibling they had not spoken to in years, a spouse they were struggling with, a child who had gone off the rails. Grief in these cases can be tangled with anger, guilt, regret and relief. People may not understand why you are so devastated, or why you are not more devastated.

David's story tells you that grief for a complicated love is still grief. It can be overwhelming even when the relationship was broken. Perhaps especially then, because now there will be no chance to mend it.

## When people hurry you

There is a hard next chapter. David's grief went on, and his soldiers, who had just won a battle for him, crept back into the city as if they had been defeated. Joab came to the king and rebuked him sharply: you have shamed all your servants. Get up and go out and speak to them.

Joab had a point about the army. David did go out. But it is striking how soon after his son's death someone told him to pull himself together.

Many grieving people know this pressure. Life does demand things of you. There may be work, children, bills and responsibilities that will not wait. Sometimes you will have to go out and sit at the gate, as David did. But doing what must be done is not the same as being finished grieving. David went out to his people. He did not stop being a father who had lost his son.

## If you have regrets

If your grief is tangled with things you wish you had said or done, it may help to say them now, to God. Some people write a letter to the person who died. Some say aloud what they wish they had said. It does not undo the past. But it can let the words out of your chest.

If the relationship was harmful, you do not have to pretend it was not. You can grieve what was, and what never was, and what now will never be.

## When you are ready

Is your grief tangled with anything complicated: anger, regret, guilt or relief? Can you tell God about it?

*God, my grief is not simple. You heard David cry for his son. Hear me too, with everything that is tangled in my heart. Amen.*
