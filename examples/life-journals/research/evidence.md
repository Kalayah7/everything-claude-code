# Evidence file: what the research says, and what we may claim

Gathered 10 to 13 November 2026 by web search. **Read this first:** the research sites
themselves (university libraries, journals, cdc.gov, pewresearch.org) are blocked from the
session that wrote this file, so every figure below comes from search summaries, press
releases and abstracts, not from the full text. Open each link and confirm the number before
you quote it in public.

## 1. What the three journals are built on

| Journal | The idea it rests on | Strongest support |
|---|---|---|
| Money on My Mind | Money trouble and poor mental health feed each other, and avoiding the numbers makes both worse | Richardson, Elliott and Roberts 2013; Richardson 2016; Zhang and Kim 2018 |
| Money, Step by Step | Teaching money skills at the moment a person needs them changes what they do | Kaiser and Menkhoff 2017; Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff and Urban 2020 |
| Not Just Me | High school stress is common, and writing about feelings gives a small, real benefit | CDC YRBS 2023; Pew 2025; Travagin, Margola and Revenson 2015 |

## 2. Dissertations and theses

Full text is free at each link. They could not be downloaded from this session, so they are
listed here for you to open.

1. **Richardson, T. (2013).** *Student Mental Health: A Prospective Cohort Study of the Impact
   of Increased Tuition Fees.* Doctorate in Clinical Psychology, University of Southampton.
   https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/358495
   Followed British students as fees tripled. Reported finding: no lasting harm from the size
   of the fee itself. What mattered more was whether students could pay their bills.
   *Use for:* Money on My Mind, and Step 2 (college) of Money, Step by Step.
2. **Sanou, S. (2024).** *Three Essays on the Impact of Debt on Young American Adults' Lives
   after Graduation.* PhD, Northern Illinois University.
   https://huskiecommons.lib.niu.edu/allgraduate-thesesdissertations/7983/
   Uses a long-running national survey of young people. Reported finding: student debt, like
   other debt, is linked to worse mental health, and a higher income did not remove the link.
   *Use for:* Step 3 (just graduated) and Step 4 (in debt).
3. **Bevers (2020).** Psychology dissertation on debt and psychological and social
   well-being, University of Texas at Arlington. Title and findings not confirmed.
   https://mavmatrix.uta.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1133&context=psychology_dissertations
4. **Sholin, T. (2022).** *The Impact of Culture on Money Attitudes: Understanding Honor, Face,
   and Dignity and Their Relation to Money Scripts.* PhD, Kansas State University.
   https://krex.k-state.edu/items/eb1573f8-c30c-42fb-9253-ef72c1f2d7dd/full
   About "money scripts": beliefs about money learned early in life. *Use for:* Money on My
   Mind, day 4 (Where I learned it).
5. **Stueve (2021).** *How Perceptions May Influence Financial Help-Seeking from Four Types of
   Financial Professionals.* PhD, Kansas State University. https://krex.k-state.edu/handle/2097/41339
   *Use for:* the "asking for help" pages. Findings not confirmed.
6. **University of Tennessee graduate dissertation.** *Exploring Students' Experiences with
   Expressive Writing.* Author not confirmed.
   https://trace.tennessee.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=7317&context=utk_graddiss
   College students describing what writing about feelings was like. *Use for:* how the
   journals are described.
7. **Minnesota State University Moorhead master's thesis** on journaling with high school
   sophomores. Title not confirmed. Reported finding: mixed results on managing emotions, but
   students asked to keep journaling after the study ended.
   https://red.mnstate.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1619&context=thesis
   *Use for:* Not Just Me.
8. **Bryant University master's thesis (2019).** *Information Avoidance in Risky Financial
   Behavior.* https://digitalcommons.bryant.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1005&context=macomm
   College students avoiding information about their own money. *Use for:* Money on My Mind,
   day 2 (What I avoid).
9. **California State University, San Bernardino.** *Mental Health Impact of Student Loan Debt
   on ...* This is a study proposal, not a finished study. Do not cite it as a finding.
   https://scholarworks.lib.csusb.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3211&context=etd

To find more, search ProQuest Dissertations and Theses (free through most public and college
libraries) for "financial anxiety", "financial therapy", "expressive writing adolescents" and
"academic stress high school".

## 3. Published studies

**Money and mental health**
- Richardson, Elliott and Roberts (2013), *Clinical Psychology Review* 33(8). Review of 65
  studies. People with unsecured debt were about three times as likely to have a mental
  health problem (odds ratio 3.24) and close to three times as likely to have depression
  (2.77). The authors stress that this shows a link, not which causes which.
  https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/359763
- Richardson and others (2016), *Community Mental Health Journal*. British students followed
  through first year. Money trouble predicted worse anxiety later, and worse mental health
  predicted more money trouble later: the "two-way street" on day 6.
  https://www.southampton.ac.uk/psychology/news/2016/08/09-financial-worries-linked-to-mental-health.page
- Zhang and Kim (2018), *Journal of Family and Economic Issues*. Americans aged 18 to 28. Each
  extra $1,000 of student loan debt went with about 6% higher odds of distress, and each
  extra $1,000 of card debt with about 4%.
- Study of United States adults aged 18 to 26 (National Health Interview Survey data): 17%
  reported high financial stress, and it was linked to psychological distress.
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11752891/
- Money and Mental Health Policy Institute (UK): 46% of people in problem debt also have a
  mental health problem. https://www.moneyandmentalhealth.org/debt-mentalhealth-facts-pressrelease/
- American Psychological Association, Stress in America 2022: 65% of adults said money was a
  significant source of stress.
  https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/stress/2022/infographic-money-economy
- Ellucian 2024 survey of 1,500 students: 59% had thought about dropping out because of money
  stress. This is a company survey, so treat it as weaker evidence.
  https://www.ellucian.com/newsroom/national-survey-reveals-59-college-students-considered-dropping-out-due-financial-stress
- Klontz, Britt, Mentzer and Klontz (2011), *Journal of Financial Therapy*: the first measure
  of "money scripts".

**Does teaching money skills work?**
- Kaiser and Menkhoff (2017), *World Bank Economic Review*. 126 studies. Financial education
  has a small positive effect on what people do. It works better when it is given at a
  "teachable moment", and debt habits are the hardest to change.
  https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:wbecrv:v:31:y:2017:i:3:p:611-630.
- Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff and Urban (2020). 76 randomized experiments, over 160,000 people.
  Positive effects on knowledge and on behavior.
- Background figures: average federal student loan balance about $39,600 per borrower
  (December 2025); average credit card rate about 21% (2026); 26 to 30 states require a
  stand-alone personal finance course to graduate. These came from secondary reports.

**Teenagers**
- CDC Youth Risk Behavior Survey 2023: 39.7% of high school students felt persistently sad or
  hopeless, 28.5% reported poor mental health, 19% were bullied at school, 16% online, and
  about three quarters slept under 8 hours. https://www.cdc.gov/yrbs/dstr/index.html
- Pew Research Center (March 2025): 68% of teens feel pressure to get good grades. Anxiety and
  depression topped the list of problems teens see at their school.
  https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2025/03/13/pressures-teens-are-facing/
- Pew Research Center (April 2025): 48% of teens say social media is mostly bad for people
  their age, and 45% say they spend too much time on it.
  https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/04/22/teens-social-media-and-mental-health/

**Does journaling work?**
- Travagin, Margola and Revenson (2015). 21 studies of expressive writing with ages 10 to 18.
  A small positive effect (g = 0.13). The authors call it "promising but not decisive".
  https://www.pubmed.gov/25656314/
- Guo (2023), *British Journal of Clinical Psychology*. 31 trials, 4,012 adults. A small
  reduction in depression, anxiety and stress, stronger when sessions were 1 to 3 days apart.
- Ramirez and Beilock (2011), *Science*: writing about worries just before an exam improved
  scores, most for the most anxious students.
- Study of 93 Virginia high school students: 15 minutes of writing on 3 days lowered general
  and math anxiety. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1112175

## 4. What we may say, and what we may not

**We may say**
- "Built on research into money stress and mental health" (and name the studies).
- "Money stress and mental health are closely linked, and each can make the other worse."
- "Studies find that writing about your feelings gives a small but real benefit for many people."
- "4 in 10 high school students say they have felt sad or hopeless for weeks at a time (CDC, 2023)."

**We may not say**
- That a journal treats, cures, prevents or reduces anxiety, depression or any condition.
- "Clinically proven", "therapist approved", "doctor recommended", or "evidence-based
  treatment". No clinician has reviewed these journals.
- That debt causes mental illness. The studies show a link.
- That the money journal will get anyone out of debt or raise a credit score.
- Any review, sales count or testimonial that is not real.

The honest pitch is this: the problem is real and well studied, the tools inside are ones
researchers and counselors commonly use, and the journal gives a person a private place to
start. The research does not support claiming more.
